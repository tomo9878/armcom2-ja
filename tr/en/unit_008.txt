Kubelwagen (HUN)	"The Volkswagen Type 82 'Kubelwagen' was a popular command car and often transported German officers throughout the war. It had a great off-road performance thanks to high ground clearance and a completely flat underside which allowed it to essentially slide over rough terrain without getting bogged down. In the final years of the war, some vehicles were provided to Hungarian officers."
Horch 108 (HUN)	"This was the larger version of the Horch 901 staff car, used primarily to transport small teams around the battlefield. It was also at times used to tow light anti-tank guns and anti-aircraft guns. In total, around 8000 were produced between 1937 and 1942. After its production ended, some vehicles were delivered to Hungary and Romania."
Opel Blitz 3000 (HUN)	"The Opel Blitz was the motorized transport backbone of the German army, and was produced in far greater numbers than any other German truck. After Hungary ended up as the last other remaining Axis state in Europe, some of these trucks were provided to the Hungarian military alongside other equipment deliveries."
Chevrolet 4x2 RD (POL)	"This is the older version of the Chevrolet military truck, with just a rear wheel drive. It was exported to nations with limited domestic industry, where it was still used during the early years of the Second World War."

M3L	"M3L was the Soviet designation for Lend-Lease M3A1 Stuart light tanks, supplied by America."
M3S	"This was the Soviet designation for Lend-Lease M3 Lee tanks."
M4	"This was the Soviet designation for Lend-Lease M4A2 medium tanks. Some 2000 with the 75mm gun were supplied to the Soviet Union during the course of the war."
M4(76)	"This was the Soviet designation for Lend-Lease M4A2(76)W Sherman tanks. They were shipped to the Soviet Union starting in May 1944 and were the only Lend-Lease Sherman variant with a three-inch main gun, wet stowage, and a gyrostabilizer."
SU-57	"Armed with an American version of the British OQF 6-pounder anti-tank gun, the SU-57 was the Soviet designation for the T48 Gun Motor Carriage, which was originally designed for the North African theatre but arrived too late to participate in the Tunisian campaign. It was instead provided to the Soviet Union as a Lend-Lease vehicle and deployed on the Eastern Front beginning in late 1943."
M10 (USSR)	"The Soviet Union received 52 M10s through Lend-Lease in 1944, which were divided into two self-propelled artillery regiments."
M5 half-track	"This American half-track is armed with a machine gun in the passenger compartment. Starting in 1943, several hundred vehicles of this type were delivered to the USSR and UK through the Lend-Lease programme."
M17 MGMC	"The M17 Multiple Gun Motor Carriage was an M5 half-track armed with a quad anti-aircraft machine gun. Production started in December 1943 and about 1,000 vehicles of this type were delivered to the USSR through the Lend-Lease programme before the end of World War Two."

M2A4 Light Tank	"The M2 Light Tank was an interwar design that led to the development of the M3 Stuart tank. It was only used in combat by the 1st Tank Battalion of the US Marine Corps, and by British forces in Burma."
M3A1 Stuart	"Compared to the M3, the M3A1 Stuart was equipped with a more powerful engine, and had a few other minor improvements such as a higher mount for the anti-aircraft machine gun."
M3A1 Satan	"Converted from the M3A1 Stuart, the Satan was a rare flame thrower tank used by the US Marine Corps in the Pacific. The 37mm main gun was replaced with a Ronson F.U.L. Mk. IV flame thrower. The M3A1 Satan was first deployed during the landings on Saipan in June 1944."
M3A3 Stuart	"The M3A3 Stuart was designed alongside the M5 and benefitted from a new sloped hull armour design. It was also roomier and carried additional ammunition as well as radio equipment."
M5A1 Stuart	"The M5A1 was produced alongside the M3, and by 1943 would be the most common variant of Stuart tank in American service. It has slightly improved armour compared to the M3. By 1944 it was being outclassed by most German tanks, and was used primarily for scouting purposes."
M24 Chaffee	"The M24 Chaffee Light Tank entered service in December 1944 and was intended to replace the remaining M5 Stuarts. Despite its light armour, it was very popular among the troops because of its mobility, firepower and reliability. It continued to serve in multiple armies around the world long after the end of the war."
M3 Lee	"With its hull-mounted 75mm gun, the M3 Lee brought some much-needed firepower to the Allied forces in North Africa. Some M3s were still used during the invasion of Sicily, but their high profile, insufficient armour and inability to fire the 75mm gun while Hull Down soon led to the decision to replace them with M4 Sherman tanks."
M3A1 Lee	"The M3A1 Lee was a slight improvement over the original M3 variant. It had a cast hull with slightly improved armour. Only about 300 tanks of this type were produced."
M3A3 Lee	"The M3A3 Lee received an engine upgrade compared to earlier variants. Only about 300 tanks of this variant were produced."
M3A4 Lee	"This extremely rare variant of the M3 Lee was equipped with a powerful Chrysler A57 multibank engine, giving it far better reliability and speed than the earlier variants."
M3A5 Lee	"The M3A5 was the final variant of the Lee. It used the same engine as the M3A3, but had a riveted hull with slightly more effective armour. About 600 M3A5 Lee tanks were produced. Most of them were used by British forces."
M4 Sherman	"First designed in 1940, the M4 'Sherman' tank entered production in 1942 and became the main battle tank for Allied forces in the war. This is the welded hull design with a vision cupola for the commander."
M4A1 Sherman	"The M4A1 was the second version of the M4 design, but it ended up entering production first in February 1942. It has a rounded, cast hull, giving it extra protection on the front. This version has a vision cupola for the commander."
