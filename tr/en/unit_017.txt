Type 94 Te-Ke	"Designed in the early 1930s, the Type 94 Te-Ke was the first mass-produced Japanese tankette. It was armed with the powerful but slow and unreliable Type 92 7.7mm machine gun, and saw combat throughout East Asia up to the end of World War Two."
Type 95 So-Ki	"This vehicle was designed to patrol railway lines in Manchuria, and has a dual-drive system that allows it to run on different gauges of railway, as well as use its own tracked drive. It has no fixed weapons, but the commander can fire an LMG from a port on the turret. It can carry up to five passengers. It saw action in China and Burma."
Type 97 Te-Ke (MG)	"The Type 97 was meant to replace the Type 94 Te-Ke tankette but often ended up serving alongside it. Since its intended 37mm main gun was in short supply, this variant was armed with a powerful but slow and unreliable Type 92 7.7mm machine gun instead. Both variants were used throughout East Asia and the South Pacific."
Type 97 Te-Ke (37mm)	"The Type 97 was meant to replace the Type 94 Te-Ke tankette, but often ended up serving alongside it. The 37mm gun of this variant was an unusually powerful weapon for a tankette. Both variants were used throughout East Asia and the South Pacific."
Type 95 Ha-Go	"The Type 95 Ha-Go was the most common Japanese tank during World War Two. It was designed to be a fast and only lightly armoured infantry support tank. One of its most distinctive characteristics was the fact that the turret machine gun was placed in a five-o'clock position."
Type 89 I-Go	"Designed in the 1920s, the Type 89 I-Go (or Chi-Ro) was a medium infantry support tank. The 57mm main gun was effective against infantry and gun emplacements, but lacked armour-piercing power until the later stages of World War Two. One of the two 6.5mm machine guns was placed in the back of the turret."
Type 91 SS-Ki	"Based on the chassis of the Type 89 I-Go medium tank, the Type 91 Soko Sagyo Ki was a multi-purpose engineering vehicle. This variant is equipped with two flame throwers and a machine gun."
Type 97 Chi-Ha	"The Type 97 Chi-Ha had the highest production numbers of all Japanese medium tanks in World War Two. Like most Japanese tanks, it was designed for an infantry support role. The low-velocity 57mm main gun, however, was ineffective against most Allied tanks that it encountered."
Type 97 Chi-Ha (late)	"The Type 97 Chi-Ha had the highest production numbers of all Japanese medium tanks in World War Two. Like most Japanese tanks, it was designed for an infantry support role. Type 1 Ho-Ni I	"Designed as a tank destroyer and self-propelled artillery vehicle, the Ho-Ni was intended to take on the M4 Sherman. It combined a Type 97 chassis with a 75mm Type 90 gun mounted on the turret ring, protected from three sides by armour plate. It was deployed in China and on Luzon in the Philippines."
Type 4 Ho-Ro	"Originally based on the German Grille series of self-propelled guns, the Ho-Ro was meant to provide the army with mechanized heavy artillery. Only a few were ever produced, and were rushed into service in late 1944 to defend the Philippines and Okinawa."
