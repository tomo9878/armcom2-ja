Sd. Kfz. 9 (ROM)	"The Sonderkraftfahrzeug 9 was the largest and most capable half-track prime mover used by Germany during the war. It was capable of towing not just artillery, but also tanks as heavy as Panzer IV. Around 2,500 were produced throughout the war, and some were delivered to Romania."
Sd. Kfz. 10	"The Sonderkraftfahrzeug 10 was a half-tracked vehicle designed for towing guns and trailers that saw widespread use throughout the war. Over 14,000 were built throughout the war, including several thousand specialized vehicles based on the same basic hull."
37M Hansa-Lloyd	"The Sonderkraftfahrzeug 7 was a half-tracked vehicle capable of towing the majority of the German artillery pieces, including the 88mm anti-aircraft gun. It was also frequently used as a recovery vehicle for damaged tanks. By 1939, 76 were exported to Hungary, where they were used under this designation."
Breda 61	"This was a licensed Italian copy of the German Sonderkraftfahrzeug 7 half-track gun tractor. It wasn't deployed until near the end of the Italian participation in the war, and most of these vehicles were ultimately seized and redeployed after the German occupation."
RSO	"Raupenschlepper Ost was developed in 1942 on the basis of tracked Soviet artillery tractors, in response to the inadequate performance of their German equivalents on the Eastern Front. In spite of its name, it was soon deployed on the Western Front as well, and around 23,000 were produced in total."
RSO (ROM)	"Raupenschlepper Ost was developed in 1942 on the basis of tracked Soviet artillery tractors, in response to the inadequate performance of their German equivalents on the Eastern Front. Around 23,000 were produced in total, and 100 were exported to Romania in 1943."
Krupp Protze	"Krupp Protze was extensively produced before the war as a tractor for light anti-tank guns and anti-aircraft guns. Around 7000 were produced between 1934 and 1941, when their production ceased because the weapons they were intended to tow were no longer in demand."
37M Krupp-Protze	"Krupp Protze was extensively produced in Germany before the war as a tractor for light anti-tank guns and anti-aircraft guns. Up to 150 were exported to Hungary in 1937 and 1938."
AS 35(t)	"Even before the war, the production of Panzer 35(t) was halted in favour of the superior 38(t). By 1942, most of the remaining vehicles were exported to the other Axis states like Romania, but around 150 tanks were also converted into armoured tractors for the super-heavy mortars."
Volkswagen Type 166	"This was an amphibious off-road staff car. It was designed after the Poland campaign, and first deployed in 1941. 14,000 were produced up until August 1944, when the Volkswagen factory at Fallersleben was destroyed in an air raid."
Opel Blitz 3000	"The Blitz truck, produced by the General Motors subsidiary company Opel, provided the motorized transport backbone for the German army. This 3-ton variant is the most common variant in use. It is the fastest wartime truck in its payload category."
Buessing-NAG 4500	"Buessing-NAG was the primary manufacturer of the Einheitsdiesel truck, and after the project's cessation in 1940, it pivoted to develop this heavy 4.5 ton truck, which entered service in 1942."
Steyr 1500A-1	"This all-wheel drive vehicle from the Austrian manufacturer Steyr did not enter production until September 1941. Once in service, it was sought after for its mobility and reliability, and nearly 18,000 were produced in total. 1500A-1 was a troop carrier variant, as opposed to the more common prime mover version."
Sd. Kfz. 3	"By the end of 1941, German trucks struggled with rasputitsa conditions on the Eastern Front. Half-track APCs had performed far better, and it was decided to replace the rear axle on some Opel Blitz truck bodies with the unneeded tracks from Panzer Is and IIs. Around 20,000 conversions were produced, most in 1943 and 1944. Their nickname means 'Mule' in German."
Sd. Kfz. 247 A	"In 1936, the Wehrmacht was interested in fielding an armoured command car, and mounted an armoured hull onto the Krupp Protze chassis. That vehicle already struggled with off-road mobility, and the extra weight only made it worse. Only 10 vehicles were delivered in 1938 before the project was cancelled. They were deployed during the invasions of Poland, France, and the Soviet Union."
Sd. Kfz. 247 B	"While the original Sd. Kfz. 247 design failed in 1938, losses on the Eastern Front forced the Wehrmacht to revisit the idea of an unarmed, armoured command car. This time, a smaller, four-wheel drive chassis with better performance was chosen. Only 58 vehicles were deployed on the Eastern Front before half-track vehicles were deemed superior for that theatre. Some remaining units were also in Normandy."
SPW 252	"This modification of the famous armoured halftrack was intended to carry shells instead of soldiers, and it had the same advantages of protection vs. small-arms fire and greater off-road mobility than its wheeled alternatives. However, it also shared the same half-track flaw of high fuel consumption, so only around 400 were produced in 1941 and 1942, and they were generally attached only to StuG units."
Munitionsschlepper I	"After the Panzer I had clearly become obsolete, there were still efforts to repurpose its chassis. They included removing the turret from some vehicles to convert them into armoured munition carriers."
Munitionsschlepper II	"As the Panzer II light tanks became less and less useful on the frontline, different uses had to be assigned to their chassis. Some were converted into armoured munition carriers, but this practice had all but stopped after the success of Marder II tank destroyers and Wespe assault guns."
Munitionspanzer III	"By 1942, it was clear that the Panzer III struggled to take on stronger Soviet tanks, and that this disparity would only grow. Some of the unwanted tanks were thus converted into armoured ammunition carriers. Unlike the conversions based on lighter tanks, they were still armed with a single bow machine gun, and so they were described as a Munitionspanzer rather than a Munitionsschlepper."
Munitionspanzer IV	"A dozen damaged Panzer IV tanks of older varieties were already converted to armoured munition carriers ahead of Operation Barbarossa. This practice expanded after the chassis became more widely available, and continued throughout the entire war. A bow machine gun was retained on these vehicles, and they so they were called Munitionspanzer rather than a Munitionsschlepper."
Munitionspanzer 38	"The Grille assault gun was a successful conversion of the aging Pz. 38(t) chassis, but it suffered from a low ammo capacity. Thus, some conversions like this one did not have the gun installed and were used exclusively to carry ammo."
Munitionsschlepper 39H	"Hundreds of French Hotchkiss tanks fell into German hands after the Battle of France. The many uses for the chassis included repurposing them into armoured munition carriers."
Opel Blitz 3000 (USSR)	"The Opel Blitz was the motorized transport backbone of the German army, and was produced in far greater numbers than any other German truck. Some inevitably ended up captured on the Eastern Front, with the number of trophies going into thousands after 1943."
Buessing-NAG 4500 (USSR)	"This heavy German truck used diesel rather than the petrol of the majority of the German war machine, and so it was produced in relatively small numbers and mainly deployed on the Eastern Front, where some trucks ended up captured and reused in Soviet service."
TGB m/39	"The Tempo Vidal G1200 was designed in Germany as a light scout and command car, but its dual-engine, all-wheel drive construction was complex and relatively few were produced compared to the faster, simpler Kubelwagen. On the other hand, the Finnish military was greatly impressed by their mobility in rough terrain, and it purchased over a hundred of these vehicles."
Tempo Vidal G1200 (ROM)	"The Tempo Vidal G1200 was designed in 1937 as a light scout and command car. Its dual-engine, all-wheel drive construction with a spare set of middle wheels provided excellent off-road mobility, but it was costly to produce and few were made compared to the faster, simpler Kubelwagen, and all production stopped in 1943. These vehicles had performed best on the Eastern Front, and some were sold to Romania."
Horch 901 (ROM)	"The Horch 901 was the second most-widely produced German staff car after the Kubelwagen, with nearly 20,000 produced between 1936 and 1943. While slower, it was much more comfortable and could carry more people and larger payloads. In 1942, Romania purchased around 300 vehicles."
Kubelwagen (ROM)	"The Volkswagen Type 82 'Kubelwagen' was a popular command car and often transported German officers throughout the war. It had great off-road performance thanks to high ground clearance and a completely flat underside which allowed it to essentially slide over rough terrain without getting bogged down. Some vehicles were eventually provided to Romania, not long before they switched sides."
Horch 108 (ROM)	"This was a larger version of the Horch 901 staff car, used primarily to transport small teams around the battlefield. It was also used to tow light anti-tank guns and anti-aircraft guns. In total, around 8000 were produced between 1937 and 1942. After its production ended, some vehicles were delivered to Hungary and Romania."
